## The short answer

**The device runs from 6 × AA, or from any USB-C charger.** Nothing is
charged inside the device (EDR-18). On a USB-C charger (an 18 W phone charger
is plenty) it runs continuously, and cells left in the holder become the
backup (EDR-20).

On the cells, in ECO mode - particles every hour, CO2 every 5 min,
temperature, humidity and VOC every 10 s - with a 25 % engineering margin:

| cells | runtime |
|---|---|
| **Energizer Ultimate Lithium L91** (recommended) | **3.5 months** (2.9 in the worst case) |
| eneloop pro (NiMH, charged outside) | 2.1 months |
| alkaline | 1.8 months |
| 1.5 V Li-ion with USB-C | 1.6 months - not recommended |

The three-month target is met with L91 cells at typical currents. With NiMH
or alkaline cells, a particle window every 2 h gives 3.0 and 2.7 months.

**Two corrections, for honesty.** The v1.3 proposal quoted 4.3 / 2.5 / 2.6
months before the model charged the regulator's quiescent current (< 0.2 mA,
Pololu's only figure) at the full pack voltage; v1.3 then said 3.7 / 2.2 /
2.1. v1.3.1 adds the undervoltage lockout (EDR-20): it switches the device
off at 1.0 V per cell so no cell can be reversed or start to leak, and its
resistors cost about 8 % of the ECO budget. Alkaline cells lose a little more,
because the lockout stops them at 1.0 V instead of 0.9 V per cell.

## On external power

With the USB-C power socket in use the device runs in CONTINUOUS mode from the charger. Cells left in the holder are the backup: the ideal diode switches to them without a gap when the power goes. Meanwhile they only lose the regulator's quiescent current, the lockout and divider resistors, and their own self-discharge:

| cells | backup still usable after |
|---|---|
| Energizer Ultimate Lithium L91 | 15.5 months |
| Panasonic eneloop pro (NiMH) | 10.1 months |
| Alkaline (Energizer E91 class) | 9.4 months |
| 1.5 V Li-ion AA with USB-C | 6.5 months |

For permanent mains operation the holder can also stay empty.

## Why the SEN62 sets the pace

One 60 s SEN62 window costs about 5.5 mWh at the cells. At one window an
hour that is **133 mWh a day, two thirds of the ECO budget**. The CO2 sensor,
in contrast, costs well under 1 mWh a day even every 5 minutes - the Sunrise
takes 1.6 mC per measurement - so CO2 no longer has to share the particle
clock (it did in v1.2).

The honest limitation: a short particle event that produces no VOC
(sweeping, a dusty draught) can fall between two hourly samples. Printing
PLA, PETG, ABS or ASA raises the VOC index first, and that switches the
device to ACTIVE.

| Pololu S9V11E2A | **vendor figure** "< 0.2 mA for most combinations"; no light-load curve published |
| cell energies | **datasheets**; the 1.5 V Li-ion line is an assumption from independent tests |
| dashboard reads | **assumption**: 96 a day at 10 poll-equivalents each |

Nothing here has been measured on an assembled AIR CHECK. Measure first
(TESTING.md, T-E0..T-E3) - the regulator's quiescent current with a µA meter
in the pack lead, everything else with a PPK2 in the 3.9 V line between the
regulator and the ideal diode (its ammeter mode stops at 5 V, below the pack
voltage):

0. **Regulator quiescent**, output unloaded: < 0.2 mA per Pololu.
1. **Idle floor**, SEN62 off, Thread attached. The model says about
   3.5 mW at the cells (≈ 0.40 mA at 8.7 V), of which the regulator's
   quiescent current and the lockout resistors are the biggest part.
2. **One full ECO hour**, SEN62 window included: model 9.0 mWh.
3. **One SEN62 window** on its own: model 5.5 mWh at the cells.

If the regulator's quiescent current comes in worse, a 2 h particle window
recovers it without any hardware change.
